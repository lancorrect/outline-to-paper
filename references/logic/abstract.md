# Writing logic: Abstract

Each item gives what to write and what to check.

The abstract is a compressed version of the chain of the introduction. The usual chain is:

| Link | Role | Required? |
|---|---|---|
| Background or problem | One or two sentences on the object of study, arriving at the general problem | Required; the background may be dropped in favor of stating the problem directly |
| Gaps of prior work | Why existing approaches have not solved the general problem | Required |
| Challenges or observation | The basis for the paper's method | Optional; write it only when there are a few clear difficulties, or when the method comes from a finding |
| Positioning | What the method is and what it builds on | Required |
| Components | What each component relies on and what it does | Required for methods with several components |
| Results | The compared methods, the scope and the direction | Required |
| Last sentence | The benefit the work brings | See "Last sentence ties back to the title" |

## Background and general problem

- What to write: One or two sentences of background, which may turn to the problem within the same sentence; or open directly with the problem. The general problem is the same as in the introduction.
- Check: The background does not trace the development of the field. The general problem is phrased as in the introduction.

## Gaps of prior work

- What to write: Summarize the gaps of prior work grouped by approach, without naming specific papers. With two lines of work, one sentence each is fine.
- Check: The gaps are phrased as in the introduction. When the criticism focuses on one point (cost, for example), the positioning sentence that follows must answer it.

## Challenges (if any)

- What to write: With several challenges, either list them after a colon, one sentence each, or pair each with its component in one sentence (the first half states the challenge, the second half how the component solves it).
- Check: Each challenge states its problem clearly enough to be understood without reading the introduction. The number of challenges matches the introduction.

## Positioning and components

- What to write: The positioning sentence states what the method is, the setting it works in and what it builds on. Each component gets one sentence, or the components are listed together, stating what each relies on and what it decides or does.
- Check: What the method builds on is credited to its authors, not presented as the paper's own. The components match the introduction and the method section.

## Results

- What to write: State the compared methods, the scope of the experiments and the direction of the results.
- Check: Not reduced to empty words such as "effective". The strength of the claim matches the experiments section. The overall result is not presented as validating every component.

## Last sentence ties back to the title

- What to write: After the experimental results, state the benefit the work brings, tied back to the title and the general problem.
- Check: The benefit comes from the method itself; no new capability is added.

## Overall check of the abstract

- The general problem, the challenges and the components match the introduction, only compressed.
- A reader learns the problem, the approach and the results without reading the paper.
- The abstract, the contributions of the introduction and the conclusion may reuse the same facts, but each does a different job: the abstract presents the problem and the approach, and the conclusion draws together what was achieved.

## Decide before writing

- Whether the formal challenge names go into the abstract
- Whether the components get abbreviations
- Whether the results are reported with numbers
- Whether to include a sentence on code availability
- The word limit
